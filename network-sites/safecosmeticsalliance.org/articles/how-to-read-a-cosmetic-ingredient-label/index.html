How to Read a Cosmetic Ingredient Label

July 15, 2026

Medically reviewed by Sarah Hitchcox, RN, BSN

An ingredient list looks like a wall of chemistry, but it follows a structure. Once you know the structure, you can read a label in about fifteen seconds and know most of what it can tell you.

The order is not random

Ingredients present at more than one percent must be listed in descending order by amount. The first item is the largest part of the formula. For most water-based products that is water, listed as Water or Aqua.

Below one percent, order no longer means anything. Manufacturers may list those ingredients in any sequence, and colorants may be listed last regardless of amount. This is the single most useful fact on this page. A brand can put a celebrated active at the end of the list and still advertise it, because at that point the list says nothing about quantity.

Finding the one percent line

There is no marker for it, but you can estimate. Preservatives and thickeners are used at low levels, so once you reach names like phenoxyethanol, sodium benzoate, xanthan gum, disodium EDTA, tocopherol, or a run of plant extracts, you are usually near or below one percent. Anything after that point is present in a small amount. That is not automatically bad. Many actives work at low concentration, and some, such as certain preservatives, are capped by law.

INCI names

Labels use the International Nomenclature of Cosmetic Ingredients, a standard naming system. It is why a label says Tocopherol instead of vitamin E, Butyrospermum Parkii Butter instead of shea butter, and Ascorbic Acid instead of vitamin C. Plants get Latin binomials. The system exists so the same substance carries the same name everywhere, which is a genuine consumer benefit even though the names look intimidating.

Fragrance is a category, not an ingredient

“Fragrance” or “Parfum” can represent a blend of many separate aroma chemicals, disclosed as one word under trade-secret protections. If you react to scented products and cannot work out why, this is usually the reason. Two products can both say Fragrance and share nothing.

MoCRA, the 2022 US cosmetics law, directed the FDA to require disclosure of certain fragrance allergens. The European Union already requires named disclosure for a defined list of allergens. If you know you react to fragrance, an unscented product is a cleaner test than a “lightly scented” one. Note that “unscented” and “fragrance-free” are not identical: an unscented product may contain a masking fragrance.

Reading the rest of the package

  • Actives panel. Products that are also drugs, such as sunscreens and acne treatments, carry a Drug Facts panel with active ingredients and their percentages. That panel gives you concentrations that a cosmetic list will not.
  • Net contents and the responsible firm. Required by federal labeling law. A product with no US address or no ingredient list at all is a warning sign.
  • Period after opening. The small open-jar icon with a number such as 12M is a European convention that many brands use worldwide. It suggests months of use after opening, not shelf life.
  • Claims are not ingredients. Words on the front of the box are marketing. The list on the back is the regulated part.

A short method

Look at the first five ingredients to learn what the product mostly is. Scan for anything you have reacted to before. Check whether the advertised hero ingredient appears near the top or trails behind the preservatives. Then, if the product is new to you, test it on a small area before you commit.

That approach works on a five-dollar drugstore cream and on the medical-grade skincare in Knoxville that a clinician might dispense. The label rules are the same. Only the concentrations and the supervision change.
