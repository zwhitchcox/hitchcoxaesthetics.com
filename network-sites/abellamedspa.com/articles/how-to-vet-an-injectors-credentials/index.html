How to Vet an Injector's Credentials

Medically reviewed by Sarah Hitchcox, RN, BSN

July 31, 2026

The person injecting your face matters more than the brand on the vial. Titles in aesthetics can be confusing on purpose, so here is how to cut through them and verify who you are dealing with.

Know the license types

In most states, these providers can legally inject neurotoxins and fillers:

  • Physicians (MD or DO): can inject independently and supervise others.
  • Nurse practitioners (NP): broad authority in many states, sometimes with physician collaboration requirements.
  • Physician assistants (PA): inject under physician supervision agreements.
  • Registered nurses (RN): inject under a physician’s or NP’s order and supervision in most states.

Rules vary by state, and estheticians are a common point of confusion. In nearly all states, estheticians cannot legally inject anything. Their training covers skin care services, not needles.

Ignore invented titles

“Master injector,” “certified injector,” and “aesthetic expert” are marketing terms. No state licensing board issues them. Some reflect a weekend course. When you see an invented title, look past it and ask for the underlying license: MD, DO, NP, PA, or RN. If the answer is anything else, do not proceed with injections.

Verify the license yourself

Every state runs a free public lookup for medical and nursing licenses. Search for your state plus “license verification.” Confirm three things:

  1. The license is active and unrestricted
  2. The name matches the person treating you
  3. There are no disciplinary actions

This takes five minutes and removes all guesswork. A surprising number of enforcement cases involve people injecting with no license at all.

Ask about supervision

For RNs and PAs, supervision is part of the legal structure. Reasonable questions include: Who is your medical director? How does the practice handle standing orders? Is a supervising provider reachable during my appointment? Good practices answer these plainly. Practices that treat supervision as a technicality tend to treat other safeguards the same way.

Ask about training and volume

A license makes injection legal. Training and repetition make it good. Useful questions:

  • How long have you been injecting?
  • Roughly how many of this specific treatment do you perform each month?
  • Where did you train, and do you pursue continuing education?
  • Can I see before-and-after photos of your own patients?

Facial anatomy expertise is the real safety margin, especially with filler, where knowing vascular anatomy prevents the most serious complications.

Ask the complication question

The single most revealing question you can ask: “What would you do if I had a vascular occlusion?” An experienced filler injector will describe recognizing the signs, stopping immediately, and using hyaluronidase, and they will confirm they stock it. An unprepared injector will minimize the question. This one answer tells you more than any diploma on the wall.

Trust the pattern, not the aesthetic

A beautiful office and a large following prove marketing skill, not clinical skill. The same checks apply whether you see a dermatologist in a big city or a nurse injector in Knoxville. Weigh the things that are hard to fake: a verifiable license, a named medical director, specific answers about training, and a real complication plan. When all of those check out, you have found an injector who respects the medicine behind the procedure. That is the person who should hold the needle.
