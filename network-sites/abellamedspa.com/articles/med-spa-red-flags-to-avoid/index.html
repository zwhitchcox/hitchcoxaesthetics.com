Med Spa Red Flags: Warning Signs to Avoid

Medically reviewed by Sarah Hitchcox, RN, BSN

July 31, 2026

Most med spas are run by careful professionals. Some are not. The difference is rarely visible in the decor or the Instagram feed, so you need to know the specific warning signs. Here are the ones that matter most.

No named medical director

Every legitimate med spa operates under a medical director, usually a physician. Their name should be easy to find, and the staff should be able to tell you who supervises care and how to reach them. If nobody can answer “who is the doctor behind this practice,” leave. Injectables are medical treatments, and someone must be accountable for them.

Nobody will say who is injecting

Ask directly: “What is your license, and who supervises you?” A qualified injector answers without hesitation. Vague titles are a red flag. “Certified injector” and “aesthetic specialist” are not licenses. The person holding the needle should be a physician, nurse practitioner, physician assistant, or registered nurse operating under proper supervision.

Prices that are too good to be true

Botox and dermal fillers have real wholesale costs. When a price falls far below the local market, something is subsidizing it. The common answers are diluted product, counterfeit product, or gray-market imports that skipped FDA oversight. Counterfeit botulinum toxin has caused hospitalizations in the United States. A bargain is not a bargain if the vial is a mystery.

You cannot see the product

A trustworthy injector will show you the box and vial, opened in front of you, with the manufacturer’s seal and lot number. Pre-filled unlabeled syringes should make you nervous. You are entitled to know exactly what is going into your face.

High-pressure sales tactics

Countdown discounts, “today only” packages, and pushy upsells belong in retail, not medicine. Pressure works against informed consent. A good practice gives you information, a clear price, and room to decide. If you feel cornered, the practice has told you what it values.

No health history or consent process

If you can walk in and get injected without answering medical questions or signing an informed consent form, the practice is skipping the parts of medicine that protect you. Screening exists to catch contraindications. Consent exists to make sure you understand risks. Neither is optional.

Treatments in homes, hotels, or “Botox parties”

Injectables require clean technique, proper storage, and the ability to manage a complication on the spot. A living room has none of that. Filler complications in particular can require emergency medication within minutes. Social settings also add alcohol and peer pressure to a medical decision, which is exactly backwards.

No plan for complications

Ask what happens if something goes wrong. A prepared practice keeps hyaluronidase on hand to dissolve filler emergencies, has a physician available for escalation, and gives you an after-hours contact. “That never happens here” is not a safety plan. It is the absence of one.

Reviews that mention only prices

Read reviews for substance. Healthy practices earn comments about assessment, communication, and natural results. If every review is about discounts and speed, the practice may be built on volume rather than care.

None of these signs alone proves a med spa is dangerous. But each one raises the odds, and several together should end the visit. You can always find another provider. You cannot always undo a bad injection.
