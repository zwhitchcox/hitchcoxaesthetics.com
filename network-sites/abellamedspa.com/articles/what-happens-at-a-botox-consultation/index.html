What Happens at a Botox Consultation

Medically reviewed by Sarah Hitchcox, RN, BSN

July 31, 2026

A good Botox consultation is a medical visit, not a sales pitch. If you know what should happen in the room, you can tell quickly whether a provider takes your safety seriously. Here is what to expect.

A real health history

Botox is a prescription medication. Before anyone talks about units or pricing, the provider should review your health history. Expect questions about:

  • Medications and supplements you take, including blood thinners
  • Neuromuscular conditions such as myasthenia gravis
  • Pregnancy or breastfeeding
  • Past cosmetic treatments and how you responded
  • Allergies and prior reactions to botulinum toxin

If a provider skips this step, that is a serious warning sign. The history is how they catch the small number of people who should not receive treatment.

A facial assessment in motion

Botox works on muscles, so the assessment should happen while your face moves. The injector should ask you to raise your brows, frown, and squint. They are mapping how your muscles pull, where your lines form, and how your face rests.

Every face is different. Brow position, muscle strength, and asymmetry all change the plan. An injector who quotes a dose before watching your face move is guessing.

A discussion of your goals

You should do a lot of the talking. What bothers you? What do you want to avoid? Many patients fear a frozen look, and a good injector will explain how dosing and placement control that. Expect honest answers about what Botox can and cannot fix. Deep static lines, volume loss, and skin texture often need different treatments.

A clear plan with numbers

Before treatment, you should hear a specific plan: which areas, roughly how many units, and what it will cost. You should also hear the timeline. Results usually start in 3 to 5 days and settle around 2 weeks. Effects typically last 3 to 4 months.

Ask who is injecting and what license they hold. In most states, an injector should be a physician, nurse practitioner, physician assistant, or registered nurse working under medical supervision. You have a right to know.

Consent and side effects

A proper consultation covers risks. Common ones include bruising, swelling, and headache. Rarer ones include eyelid droop or asymmetry, which are temporary but worth understanding. You should sign an informed consent form that lists these risks, and you should have time to read it.

No pressure to treat that day

Many med spas offer same-day treatment after a consultation, and that is fine if you feel ready. But you should never feel pushed. A provider who leans on limited-time discounts or urgency tactics is prioritizing revenue over judgment. Walking away to think is always a valid choice, and good providers say so.

Questions worth asking

Bring a short list:

  • Who is the medical director, and are they on site?
  • Which product do you use, and is it FDA approved?
  • What happens if I have a complication after hours?
  • How do you handle touch-ups if results are uneven?

The answers matter less than the reaction. A confident, unhurried answer signals a practice that has thought about safety. Defensiveness signals the opposite.

A consultation costs you little and tells you a lot. Treat it as an interview. The right injector will treat it the same way.
