Coconut Oil for Skin: Benefits and Drawbacks

Medically reviewed by Sarah Hitchcox, RN, BSN

Coconut oil is the celebrity of natural skincare. It is cheap, smells like vacation, and shows up in half the DIY recipes on the internet. It earns some of that reputation. It also causes more clogged pores than any other pantry ingredient. Here is the balanced picture.

What coconut oil is

Coconut oil is mostly saturated fat, and nearly half of that is lauric acid. Lauric acid has genuine antibacterial properties in lab studies, which fuels many of the health claims. The oil is solid below about 76 degrees Fahrenheit and melts on skin contact.

Where it genuinely helps

  • Body moisturizer. On dry shins, elbows, and heels, coconut oil is excellent. Studies on dry skin and mild eczema found it moisturizes as well as mineral oil or better.
  • Makeup remover. It dissolves even waterproof mascara. Follow with a regular cleanser so the oil does not linger.
  • Hair and cuticles. It penetrates the hair shaft better than most oils and reduces protein loss. Split ends and ragged cuticles love it.
  • Massage and scrub base. It is a fine carrier for a sugar or salt body scrub.

Where it causes trouble

Coconut oil is highly comedogenic, meaning it tends to block pores. On standard comedogenicity scales it rates 4 out of 5. On facial skin, especially oily or acne-prone skin, it commonly triggers whiteheads and breakouts a week or two after it starts feeling lovely. This is the single most common complaint we hear about it, and it is why we wrote a separate guide: Coconut Oil for Acne: Why It Usually Backfires.

It is also a weak match for very dry, cracked facial skin conditions that need barrier-repair ingredients like ceramides, and it offers no sun protection at all.

How to use it well

  1. Keep it below the neck. Body: yes. Face: only if your skin has proven it can handle it.
  2. Patch test on the inner forearm for 24 hours, like any new ingredient.
  3. Choose virgin, cold-pressed oil for skin use. Refined oil is fine for scrub bases.
  4. Use thin layers. A little melts a long way.
  5. If new bumps appear on your face after you start using it, stop. The oil is the likely cause.

The bottom line

Coconut oil is a very good body moisturizer, hair treatment, and makeup remover, and a risky facial moisturizer. Match the ingredient to the job and it earns its shelf space. If persistent breakouts or irritation are the problem, a dermatologist or a nurse-run med spa in Knoxville will get you further than the pantry.
