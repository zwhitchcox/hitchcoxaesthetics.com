Sun Damage and Daily Sunscreen

If you only ever adopt one skin habit, this is the one with the best evidence behind it.

Medically reviewed by Sarah Hitchcox, RN, BSN

Ultraviolet radiation is responsible for the majority of what people call aged skin: fine lines, deep wrinkles, brown patches, broken capillaries, and a rough leathery texture. It is also the main modifiable cause of skin cancer. Those two facts come from the same body of research, and they point to the same simple habit.

How the damage happens

Sunlight reaching the ground includes UVA and UVB. UVB is shorter, stronger, and mostly hits the outer layer. It causes sunburn and direct damage to DNA in skin cells. UVA is longer, penetrates deeper into the dermis, and breaks down the collagen and elastin that hold skin firm. UVA passes through window glass and stays relatively constant through the day and across seasons. That is why a person can accumulate real damage while never once feeling burned.

The visible effects build slowly and are cumulative. Pigment cells respond to repeated exposure by producing melanin unevenly, which is where sun spots come from. Damaged collagen is replaced with disorganized fibers, which is where crepey texture comes from.

What sunscreen does and does not do

In a randomized trial in Nambour, Australia, adults assigned to daily sunscreen showed measurably less skin aging after four and a half years than adults using it at their discretion. The same population showed reduced rates of squamous cell carcinoma and melanoma over longer follow up. That combination of a randomized design and a hard outcome is rare in skincare.

Sunscreen does not make you immune. It reduces the dose of ultraviolet light your skin receives. It works alongside shade, clothing, hats, and avoiding peak sun, not instead of them.

Practical use

  • Choose broad spectrum, SPF 30 or higher. Broad spectrum means it covers UVA as well as UVB. SPF alone only describes UVB.
  • Use enough. Most people apply a quarter to half of the tested amount, which cuts real protection sharply. For the face and neck, roughly a third of a teaspoon is a workable target.
  • Reapply. Every two hours outdoors, and after swimming, sweating, or towel drying.
  • Mineral or chemical filters both work. Zinc oxide and titanium dioxide are good options for sensitive or reactive skin. The best sunscreen is the one you will actually wear every day.
  • Do not forget the edges. Ears, the back of the neck, the hairline part, the tops of the hands, and the lower lip are common sites of both damage and skin cancer.

Tanning beds are not a safe alternative to sun. The World Health Organization classifies ultraviolet tanning devices as carcinogenic to humans. There is no such thing as a safe indoor tan.

Treating damage that is already there

Existing sun damage does not vanish, but some of it responds to treatment. Prescription retinoids improve fine lines and pigment over months. Professional chemical peels and light based devices can improve brown patches, redness, and texture, and intense pulsed light and fractional resurfacing are common tools for exactly this problem. Results and risks depend heavily on skin tone, so an in person assessment comes first. If you are exploring laser treatments in Knoxville or in your own city, ask specifically how the device settings will be adapted to your skin type.

Whatever you do about existing damage, keep the daily habit. New exposure undoes the work. And book a skin check with a dermatologist if you have many moles, a family history of melanoma, a history of blistering sunburns, or any spot that is changing.
